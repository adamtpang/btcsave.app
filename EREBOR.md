# Lessons from Erebor (Palmer Luckey's bank)

Source: Palmer Luckey on TBPN, "Why I Started My Own Bank." Erebor received its charter and went operational.

Erebor is the closest living analogue to Iron. Same shape, different asset: an incumbent blew up and took customer money (SVB for him, Celsius and BlockFi for us), the founder was the underserved customer, and the pitch is survival rather than yield. Where he lands differently from us is worth more than where he agrees.

---

## 1. The origin story is the same, and it is the strongest one there is

> "I've been looking at starting a bank for a while, primarily for my own personal use, because there weren't banks out there that really understood my business."

He built the bank he needed. Adam is the heavy credit user who pays more in interest than any other expense. Keep leading with founder-is-the-customer: it is the same engine that made Erebor credible, and it cannot be copied by an incumbent.

## 2. Survival is the product

> "How could you do it a very conservative way, very very low-risk way, you can ensure you're not going to go out of business... you can at least be the last man standing."

And the bar he sets for himself:

> "Oh, and we need to not lose their money. If we can do that, we'll be doing better than many other banks."

That is `GRAVEYARD.md` in his words. It confirms the whole Iron thesis: in a category littered with corpses, "we structurally cannot blow up" IS the differentiator, not a disclaimer. Overcollateralization and no rehypothecation are the product, not the fine print.

## 3. Positioning as a filter, not a feature (the sharpest idea in the interview)

On preemptively working with the government against fraud:

> "Which is great for me too, because it means that people who want to engage in fraud are going to go to other banks. It means that people who don't want to engage in fraud are going to be thrilled."

He designed a policy that repels the customers he does not want. He also names a villain to be the opposite of ("think of it as the opposite of HSBC where they were banking the cartels").

**Iron's version:** the graveyard rules are a filter. Being structurally unable to rehypothecate repels yield-chasers and rate-shoppers, and attracts exactly the person who got burned in 2022 and swore never again. Say the quiet part: *if you want the highest LTV and a yield account, go somewhere else, and understand what happened to the last people who did.* Our named villain is already written: Celsius and BlockFi.

## 4. Do not rent your right to exist (the decision this forces on Iron)

This is the part Iron has to actually decide on. He addresses our exact tradeoff:

> "People just avoid getting a charter at all. They want to be a fintech company and they basically will dance around the fact that realistically what they're doing is basically being a bank."

Why he took the harder path:

> "The moment that you're dependent on somebody else for the key infrastructure, the key risk management, the key licensure, they can kick you off the platform. They can be forced to kick you off the platform."

> "If you don't control your own charter, if you don't control your own destiny, the buck doesn't stop with you and you're always going to have to keep other parties happy."

He cites the Steam censorship cascade: nobody in the chain admits to being the censor, but everyone agrees that if you do not comply they drop you.

**This is graveyard rule 7 in different clothes.** WaveCrest bricked six card programs in a single day. Wirecard took the rest. Iron's current plan (Rain for issuing, multi-issuer redundancy, prefer a Visa principal member) is the right *start*, and Palmer says the honest thing about it: renting is fine until the day someone else's politics or risk committee decides your business is over.

He is also honest about the cost of owning it: a charter means you cannot hypergrow ("you can't just scale to 50 billion of deposits tomorrow even if there's the demand").

**The Iron position:** rent the rails to launch, but write down the trigger for owning them. Redundancy is not the same as sovereignty; it buys time, not control. The roadmap already ends at "Bank." This is the argument for why that stage is not optional vanity, it is the point at which Iron stops being cancellable.

## 5. Stablecoin settlement is now a mainstream bank feature

> "Using dollar-backed cryptocurrencies to have 365 24/7 settlement of payments, which is something that a lot of businesses need and very few get."

A maximally establishment-aligned founder built his bank on stablecoin rails. That kills the "why not just use dollars" objection from the other direction.

But note the warning:

> "Is there a network effect? Probably, but... pretty quickly everyone is going to realize that they need to support these things... probably most banks are going to get dragged into this."

So never build the moat on stablecoin settlement. It is table stakes on a two-year clock. Iron's moat has to stay where it is: non-custodial by construction, the card and local-rail spend layer, and the underwriting data flywheel.

## 6. The roadmap comes from customers, not from ambition

> "The products that we are prioritizing are based entirely on the customer feedback that we're getting, the things that they tell us they need."

And he stays narrow and boring on risk:

> "It's probably not going to be residential mortgages, but things like equipment financing, very conservative, where it's not going to turn into a risk management problem."

He has a charter, a war chest, and a brand, and he still says the roadmap is dictated by customers. Iron has none of those and currently has zero logged borrower conversations. This is the outside validation for IRO-1 being the constraint.

## 7. Do not create a dead-end call to action

From the Mod Retro console, on why they are stockpiling before launch:

> "We started selling them and then all of a sudden we sold out... they go to try to buy one and they can't buy one. Will they remember to come back and buy it some other time?"

Applies directly to Iron's waitlist. Driving demand to a product nobody can use burns the audience once. Either the waitlist genuinely nurtures (a real sequence, concierge contact, a reason to stay) or hold the volume until there is something to say yes to.

## 8. Two lines worth stealing

**"From innovation to extraction"** (he credits Nirav Patel of Framework):

> "There's these industries that have moved from innovation to extraction and they're now in the extraction phase where they try to make tons and tons of money and actually pushing the limits is not the top priority."

That is the credit card industry in five words, and Adam pays the extraction personally. Chase and Discover solved credit decades ago and have spent the years since optimizing the trap: 22 to 30% APR, minimum payments engineered to maximize revolve, fee cascades. Iron is a return to the innovation phase of credit. Use this framing.

**"Commercial failure doesn't mean a product's bad. It just means they couldn't figure out how to make a business out of it."**

Said about his motorcycle collection, but it reframes the graveyard perfectly: Celsius and BlockFi were not bad ideas. Borrowing against bitcoin is a real, durable need. They just could not run it without stealing the collateral. **The graveyard is proof of demand, not proof of impossibility.** That is the single best answer to "didn't this already fail?"

---

## What changes for Iron

1. **Reframe the pitch around extraction.** Credit moved from innovation to extraction; Iron moves it back. Add to the positioning.
2. **Say who Iron is not for.** Turn the graveyard rules into an explicit filter in the copy.
3. **Answer "didn't this already fail" with "the graveyard is proof of demand."**
4. **Write down the sovereignty trigger.** Rain to launch, but name the conditions under which Iron pursues its own licence rather than renting one, and put it in the roadmap between Graduate and Bank.
5. **Stop treating stablecoin settlement as a moat.** It is table stakes; the moat is non-custodial construction plus data.
6. **Do not drive waitlist volume into a dead end.** Either nurture properly or stay quiet until there is a product.
